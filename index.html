A Nation on the Wing

Tracing Australia's native birdlife across states, habitats, ecological lifestyles and conservation landscapes.

The story

Australia's birdlife tells a different story depending on how we look at it. Beyond the numbers, patterns emerge that reveal surprising similarities, regional differences and connections across the country's native bird species.

800+ bird species found across Australia
45% of Australian bird species are found nowhere else on Earth
1 in 6 Australian bird species are threatened with extinction

The big picture   /   Species richness

Where is birdlife most diverse?

Comparing the number of bird species recorded across Australian states and territories.

Context

Each total represents the number of distinct native bird species recorded in a state or territory, rather than individual birds.

At a Glance

Queensland records the highest species richness (642), more than double Tasmania's total (307).

Queensland's remarkable bird diversity is shaped by more than its size. The Wet Tropics occupy less than 0.2% of Australia's land area yet support around 40% of its bird species , demonstrating how biodiversity can be concentrated in rainforest habitats. Meanwhile, Western Australia's coastline includes Roebuck Bay, an internationally important feeding ground for migratory shorebirds.

Tasmania offers a different perspective. Despite its lower overall species richness, its geographic isolation has helped shape 12 endemic bird species found nowhere else on Earth.

Sources: Wet Tropics · Roebuck Bay · Tasmanian Birds

Island vs Mainland

Tasmania’s island and marine habitats explains its unusually high proportion of petrels and shearwaters (12.7%), while honeyeaters are prominent across much of mainland Australia.

Beyond Species Counts

Two regions can have similar species totals yet very different bird-family compositions. Looking at relative family shares reveals patterns that overall species richness cannot capture.

Map / Hexagonal bins

Reading the Pattern

Explore where some of Victoria’s most familiar native bird species are recorded. Select different birds and compare their observation patterns across 2025 and 2026. Consistent hexagon locations and colour ranges make it easier to identify similarities, differences and changes over time.

The Human Footprint

The dense cluster around Greater Melbourne extends towards regional centres such as Geelong, Ballarat and Bendigo. This pattern may reflect the concentration of people, accessible locations and citizen-science activity.

In contrast, parts of northwestern Victoria and the eastern highlands show fewer recorded observations. Remote landscapes, limited road access and lower population density may contribute to reduced reporting. Coastal destinations and popular recreational areas may attract more observers, highlighting how human movement and accessibility can influence the apparent distribution of bird sightings.

Forest Connections

Forest birds form a prominent pathway through invertebrate-based diets and insessorial (perching) lifestyles. This suggests that many forest species share similar feeding strategies and behaviours.

A closer look   /   Nesting habits

No Two Nests Are Alike

The connections between habitat and lifestyle lead to another question...

Reading the chart

Choose a bird group to compare its nesting types. Each segment represents the percentage of species associated with that nesting type; categories can overlap.

More than one way to nest

Ducks and geese show a surprisingly varied nesting pattern. While ground nesting is most common, some species also use tree hollows, supported nests and even floating sites.

The Hollow-Nesting Specialists

Parrots show a much stronger preference for tree hollows, with 37 of 42 species associated with this nesting type. This contrasts with honeyeaters, which predominantly use supported nests.

06
Physical diversity

Small birds, big differences.

Nesting habits are only one kind of variation. How do Australian birds compare in body length and weight?

Two ways of measuring size

The histogram groups individual species by average body length. The bar chart instead compares median species body mass across families with at least five measured species.

Mostly small in stature

Over 60% of Australian bird species cluster tightly between 10 cm and 40 cm in length. Evolutionary success heavily favors this mid-to-small size range, with sizes over 60 cm becoming exceptionally rare.

The marine marvels

The heaviest bird families (Penguins, Albatrosses, Cormorants, and Gannets) are all water-dependent or marine species. Aquatic adaptations and gliding efficiency allow for much higher body masses than terrestrial lifestyles.

Shared flyways   /   Migratory listings

Birds Without Borders

Explore JAMBA, CAMBA and ROKAMBA listings using counts or the share of recorded bird species.

What these circles reveal..

Larger circles represent more migratory-listed species or a greater share of recorded birdlife, depending on the selected measure.

Counts vs Share

Western Australia leads the JAMBA count with 69 listed species. However, a state with fewer listed species may have a higher percentage relative to its total bird diversity. Switching between counts and percentages reveals this distinction.

Migratory birds cross international borders, making their protection a shared responsibility. Australia has three agreements with Japan (JAMBA), China (CAMBA) and South Korea (ROKAMBA). Each supports the conservation of migratory species and their habitats.

Some migratory shorebirds travel thousands of kilometres between Arctic breeding grounds in Siberia and Alaska and Australia along the East Asian–Australasian Flyway. Coastal wetlands such as Roebuck Bay provide essential feeding and resting grounds along these journeys.

Sources: Migratory Birds · Migratory Shorebirds

From movement to risk   /   Conservation

The Fragile Side of Birdlife

Behind Australia's rich birdlife lies a growing concern for the survival of its most vulnerable species.

Reading the waffle

Each square represents about 1% of the species included in this chart. Threatened refers to EPBC-listed species, not the number of individual birds.

A bird doesn't have to be extremely rare to be threatened. Rapid population declines, shrinking habitats and restricted geographic ranges can all contribute to its conservation status. Different species face different risks, even when their populations appear relatively large.

The lighter squares don't necessarily represent species that are thriving. Habitat loss, introduced predators and changing environmental conditions can also affect birds not currently listed as threatened. The chart captures formal conservation status, but the challenges facing Australia's birdlife extend beyond these classifications.

From a national share to a state-by-state picture

A single national percentage cannot show where threatened species are concentrated. The next map compares threatened shares within each state and territory rather than treating Australia as one total.

09
A changing picture   /   Conservation

The Geography of Survival

Not all regions share the same conservation story, even within one country.

Reading the Map

State area is used for geography; colour shows the threatened species percentage — not the absolute number of birds.

The Contrast

Queensland records the greatest bird diversity, with 642 native species, yet Tasmania has the highest proportion of threatened species. Having more bird species does not necessarily mean having a greater share at risk.

Two similarly coloured states may face very different conservation challenges. This map combines Vulnerable, Endangered and Critically Endangered species into one percentage, meaning it cannot reveal how severely threatened each species is.

Take the critically endangered swift parrot . It breeds in Tasmania before migrating to mainland woodlands for winter. Habitat loss and predation by introduced sugar gliders threaten its survival, showing how protecting a single species can require conservation efforts across multiple regions.

Sources: Swift Parrot Recovery Plan · Threats and Conservation

Concentrated risk

Albatrosses stand out: 15 of the 16 species in this dataset are listed as threatened. These seabirds face serious challenges from fishing bycatch, marine pollution and changing ocean conditions, making their conservation urgent.

Beyond geography
